In addition to **[CIS RAM](https://learn.cisecurity.org/cis-ram)**, the most respected cybersecurity risk assessment frameworks and methods are below. Some are true **risk assessment methods**, some are broader **risk management frameworks**, and some are **supporting models**.

## 1. NIST SP 800-30

[NIST SP 800-30: Guide for Conducting Risk Assessments](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/30/r1/final) is one of the most respected formal risk assessment guides, especially in the United States. It is widely used in government, regulated sectors, contractors, auditors, and mature enterprise programs.

It is strong because it gives a structured process for identifying threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood, impact, and risk response.

**Best for:**

- SMEs that want a defensible, formal process  
- Government contractors  
- Regulated organizations  
- Companies that want a risk register with clear likelihood and impact logic  
- Organizations aligning with NIST-style governance

Weakness: it can feel heavy if copied directly into a small company without simplification.

Takeaway: **Use NIST SP 800-30 as the backbone for the risk assessment process, but simplify the scoring and documentation for SMEs.**

## 2. ISO/IEC 27005

[ISO/IEC 27005](https://www.iso.org/standard/80585.html) is the main ISO standard for information security risk management. It supports implementation of an information security management system under [ISO/IEC 27001](https://www.iso.org/standard/27001).

It is respected globally because it fits directly into ISO 27001 certification and information security governance.

**Best for:**

- Companies pursuing ISO 27001  
- International organizations  
- Suppliers serving enterprise customers  
- Companies that need formal risk treatment plans  
- Organizations that want risk management tied to an ISMS

Weakness: ISO language can be abstract and process-heavy. It tells you what a strong risk process should include, but it does not always give the most practical SME-level execution details.

Takeaway: **Use ISO/IEC 27005 if the company has, wants, or must align with ISO 27001.**

## 3. NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0

The [NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0](https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework) is not a pure risk assessment method, but it is one of the most respected cybersecurity risk management frameworks.

It organizes cybersecurity work around major functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. That makes it very useful for structuring a cybersecurity playbook.

**Best for:**

- Executive cybersecurity programs  
- SMEs building their first serious cybersecurity structure  
- Board-level reporting  
- Gap assessments  
- Cybersecurity maturity reviews  
- Mapping controls to business risk

Weakness: it does not, by itself, tell you exactly how to score individual risks. You still need a method like CIS RAM, NIST SP 800-30, FAIR, or ISO 27005 for risk scoring.

Takeaway: **Use NIST CSF 2.0 as the top-level structure, and use CIS RAM or NIST SP 800-30 underneath it for actual risk assessment.**

## 4. FAIR

[FAIR](https://www.fairinstitute.org/) stands for Factor Analysis of Information Risk. It is one of the most respected quantitative cyber risk models.

FAIR is different from most frameworks because it tries to express risk in financial terms instead of simple high/medium/low ratings. It breaks risk into factors such as threat event frequency, vulnerability, loss event frequency, and probable loss magnitude.

**Best for:**

- Board-level financial cyber risk conversations  
- Mature cybersecurity teams  
- Cyber insurance discussions  
- Budget justification  
- Comparing security investments  
- Quantifying ransomware, data breach, outage, and fraud scenarios

Weakness: FAIR requires better data, stronger estimation discipline, and more analytical maturity. If a small company uses FAIR casually, it may create fake precision.

Takeaway: **Use FAIR for high-value risks where financial quantification matters, not as the first framework for every SME.**

## 5. OCTAVE Allegro

[OCTAVE Allegro](https://www.sei.cmu.edu/library/introducing-octave-allegro-improving-the-information-security-risk-assessment-process/) was developed by Carnegie Mellon’s Software Engineering Institute. It is an asset-centric information security risk assessment method.

It focuses on identifying critical information assets, understanding how they are used, identifying threat scenarios, and evaluating the consequences.

**Best for:**

- Organizations that want a workshop-driven risk assessment  
- Teams with limited tooling  
- Information asset risk analysis  
- SMEs that need to understand business process risk  
- Organizations that prefer structured discussion over heavy technical scanning

Weakness: it is older and less commonly used in modern commercial cybersecurity programs than NIST, ISO, CIS, or FAIR.

Takeaway: **Use OCTAVE-style thinking for workshops and business process interviews, but do not make it the main SME framework unless the organization prefers a non-technical, asset-first approach.**

## 6. NIST Risk Management Framework

The [NIST Risk Management Framework](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/risk-management/about-rmf) is broader than a risk assessment method. It is a lifecycle for managing system risk through categorization, control selection, implementation, assessment, authorization, and continuous monitoring.

**Best for:**

- Government contractors  
- Federal systems  
- Highly regulated environments  
- Organizations needing formal system authorization  
- Security programs that must prove control implementation and monitoring

Weakness: it is too heavy for most ordinary SMEs unless they have government, defense, healthcare, financial, or compliance-driven requirements.

Takeaway: **A mature governance framework, but do not use it as the default SME method unless compliance requires it.**

## 7. ISO 31000 and IEC 31010

[ISO 31000](https://www.iso.org/iso-31000-risk-management.html) is a general enterprise risk management standard. [IEC 31010](https://www.iso.org/standard/72140.html) provides risk assessment techniques.

These are not cybersecurity-specific, but they are respected because they help align cyber risk with broader business risk management.

**Best for:**

- Enterprise risk management teams  
- Companies that want cyber risk integrated with operational, financial, legal, and strategic risk  
- Board-level risk governance  
- Organizations with mature risk committees

Weakness: they are too general if used alone. They need a cyber-specific method underneath them.

Takeaway: **Use ISO 31000 language when speaking to executives, but use CIS RAM, NIST SP 800-30, ISO 27005, or FAIR for cybersecurity-specific execution.**

## 8. ISACA COBIT and Risk IT

[COBIT](https://www.isaca.org/resources/cobit) from ISACA is a respected IT governance framework. ISACA’s older [Risk IT Framework](https://www.isaca.org/resources/risk-it-framework) is focused on IT risk governance and management.

These are useful when cybersecurity risk must be connected to IT governance, accountability, controls, and enterprise objectives.

**Best for:**

- IT governance  
- Audit-heavy organizations  
- CIO/CISO reporting  
- Control ownership  
- IT risk committees  
- Organizations already using ISACA methods

Weakness: COBIT is more governance-oriented than hands-on risk assessment. It is not usually the simplest starting point for an SME.

Takeaway: **Use COBIT for governance alignment, not as the main operational risk assessment method.**

## 9. EBIOS Risk Manager

[EBIOS Risk Manager](https://cyber.gouv.fr/en/ebios-risk-manager-method) is a respected French/European cyber risk assessment method developed by ANSSI.

It is scenario-driven and useful for understanding threat sources, business values, attack paths, and strategic cyber risk.

**Best for:**

- European organizations  
- Regulated entities  
- Critical infrastructure  
- Organizations that want strong scenario-based analysis  
- Teams assessing advanced attack paths

Weakness: it can be more complex than needed for a small company doing its first assessment.

Takeaway: **Use EBIOS RM when a company needs a more advanced, threat-scenario-driven European-style method.**

## 10. ISF IRAM2

[ISF IRAM2]([https://www.securityforum.org/solutions-and-insights/iram2-information-risk-assessment-methodology/](https://www.securityforum.org/solutions-and-insights/information-risk-assessment-methodology-2-iram2/)) is the Information Security Forum’s information risk assessment methodology. It is respected in larger enterprises and mature risk programs.

**Best for:**

- Large organizations  
- Mature information security teams  
- Enterprise risk programs  
- Organizations already involved with ISF  
- Detailed information risk analysis

Weakness: it is not as accessible for ordinary SMEs because ISF materials are generally tied to membership and enterprise use.

Takeaway: **Good for mature enterprise programs, but not the best default for an SME playbook.**

## 11. IEC 62443 for OT and industrial environments

[IEC 62443](https://www.isa.org/standards-and-publications/isa-standards/isa-iec-62443-series-of-standards) is highly respected for industrial automation and operational technology security.

It is not a general SME cyber risk framework, but it becomes very important if the company has manufacturing systems, industrial control systems, production lines, building systems, or operational technology.

**Best for:**

- Manufacturing  
- Industrial systems  
- Critical infrastructure  
- Operational technology  
- Factories  
- Energy, utilities, logistics, and production environments

Weakness: It is overkill for a normal office-only SME.

Takeaway: **Use IEC 62443 only when OT, ICS, manufacturing, or industrial systems are in scope.**

# Risk Assessment Options

For a general SME cybersecurity playbook, I would rank them this way:

1. **[CIS RAM](https://learn.cisecurity.org/cis-ram)** — best practical SME-friendly risk assessment method when paired with CIS Controls.
    
2. **[NIST SP 800-30](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/30/r1/final)** — best formal risk assessment backbone.
    
3. **[NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0](https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework)** — best overall playbook structure, but not enough by itself for scoring risk.
    
4. **[ISO/IEC 27005](https://www.iso.org/standard/80585.html)** — best if ISO 27001 alignment matters.
    
5. **[FAIR](https://www.fairinstitute.org/)** — best for financial quantification of cyber risk.
    
6. **[OCTAVE Allegro](https://www.sei.cmu.edu/library/introducing-octave-allegro-improving-the-information-security-risk-assessment-process/)** — useful for asset-focused workshops.
    
7. **[COBIT](https://www.isaca.org/resources/cobit)** — useful for IT governance and control ownership.
    
8. **[EBIOS Risk Manager](https://cyber.gouv.fr/en/ebios-risk-manager-method)** — useful for advanced scenario-based analysis.
    
9. **[IEC 62443](https://www.isa.org/standards-and-publications/isa-standards/isa-iec-62443-series-of-standards)** — essential only when OT/ICS is involved.
    

### Takeaway

**Use NIST CSF 2.0 to structure the cybersecurity program, CIS RAM or NIST SP 800-30 to conduct the risk assessment, ISO/IEC 27005 when ISO alignment is required, and FAIR only when the organization needs financial cyber risk quantification.**
